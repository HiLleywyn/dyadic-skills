# Research Brief

A research brief answers one question from evidence a reader can check. Work
through these steps in order and don't skip the planning.

## 1. Pin down the question

Restate the question in one sentence before you search. Settle:

- **Scope**: what exactly is being asked, and what isn't.
- **Time**: "now", a date range, or no time limit. Current facts need sources
  from the last few months.
- **Place**: a country, city or market, if the answer depends on it.

If the question is ambiguous in a way that changes the answer, ask one short
question first. Otherwise state the assumption you made at the top of the brief.

## 2. Plan the searches

Write down two to four searches that together cover the question, each aimed
at a different kind of source:

1. The primary source (the organization, the standard, the dataset, the
   official page) behind the claim.
2. Independent reporting or analysis of it.
3. A dissenting or critical view, when the topic is disputed.

Search once per line of the plan. Don't repeat a search with different
wording when the first one already answered it.

## 3. Read, don't skim

Search snippets are often wrong or out of date. Open the pages that matter
and read them. For each fact you keep, note:

- the claim, in your own words;
- the exact page it came from (its full address);
- the date the page was published or last updated.

Prefer primary sources. Use a secondary source only when it adds something
(a summary of several studies, an expert's reading of the data).

Match reading depth to the question. An abstract, search snippet, metadata page,
or truncated excerpt is not a full paper. If the task requires reviewing methods,
results or limitations, follow an actual full-text/HTML/PDF link returned by the
source and read the relevant sections within the available budget. Keep track
of which sections were actually retrieved; never claim a full review from an abstract.

## 4. Weigh the evidence

- When sources disagree, say so and say which is more credible and why
  (newer data, primary source, larger sample, no conflict of interest).
- Separate what the sources report from what you infer from them.
- Numbers: give the figure, its unit, its date and its source. Don't round
  away the uncertainty.
- A research gap does not establish a market gap. Check relevant existing
  products independently before saying a product does not exist. Without that
  evidence, describe a proposed opportunity as a hypothesis, not an established absence.

## 5. Write the brief

Use this shape, and keep it short: most briefs fit on one screen.

```
**Bottom line:** two or three sentences that answer the question.

**What the sources say**
- A finding, as reported by [the source's name](exact address) (month year).
- Another finding, with its own link.

**Disputed** (only when sources really disagree)
- Where they disagree, and which is more credible and why.

**Read next**
- One to three links worth the reader's time, with one line on why.
```

Rules:

- Every factual claim from a source gets an inline link to the exact page.
  Never link a homepage when you read an article, and never invent an address.
- Stable, well-known facts (definitions, geography, history) don't need a
  search; give them from what you know.
- No filler: no "great question", no summary of what you are about to do.

## 6. Check before you send

- Does the bottom line answer the question that was asked?
- Is every link one you actually opened or saw in a result?
- Did every part of the question get an answer? Search again only for a named
  essential gap within the remaining budget. Deliver the supported findings
  when an optional follow-up fails; do not restart the research or add a generic
  failure caveat. If an essential conclusion is still uncertain, scope that
  conclusion honestly instead of inventing certainty.

## Example

Question (invented for illustration): "Is the Harbourline tram extension in
Port Aldren still due to open next year?"

A good bottom line: "Probably not on time: the city's own project page now
gives the second half of next year, and the latest council minutes record a
three-month delay to track work." Each of those two claims then appears under
**What the sources say** with a link to the project page and to the minutes.
